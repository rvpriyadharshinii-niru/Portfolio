The shift

Static prototypes weren’t always enough.

For complex enterprise workflows, I could design the intended experience in Figma — but some problems only became obvious once I could actually use it.

Interaction behaviour, responsive states, navigation and longer end-to-end flows were easier to evaluate in a functioning interface than in a static one. So AI-assisted coding became part of the design process itself, not a separate experiment after it.

Before AI

Claude wasn’t the starting point.
The product problem was.

Before asking Claude to build anything, I structured the product context — what we were solving, who it was for, the jobs users needed to accomplish, key journeys, requirements, constraints and expected behaviour.

The goal wasn’t to ask AI to invent the UX. It was to give it enough structured context to help execute and test the UX I was already designing.

Design system as context

The design system became context for the AI.

Generating an interface with AI is easy. Generating something that actually belongs inside an existing enterprise product is much harder.

Our components and patterns already existed in the design system, Storybook and our codebase — so Claude could work from established product patterns instead of inventing a new UI language.

Where AI helped

From idea to something I could use.

Claude reduced the time between making a product decision and experiencing it in a functioning interface. That gave me more opportunities to evaluate workflows, behaviour and edge cases instead of repeatedly recreating static screens.

Where it still needed me

Working software isn’t necessarily good UX.

AI could implement an instruction quickly, but it couldn’t decide whether that interaction was actually right for the user. Hierarchy, workflow simplification, edge cases, responsive behaviour and whether an experience felt coherent still required design judgement.

Iteration

Build → use → notice → change.

Once an experience was functional, using it surfaced problems that are hard to see in static screens — and deciding what to do about them was still design work, not something Claude could decide on its own.

Choosing the right surface

Figma and code became two surfaces of the same design process.

For fast visual exploration and collaborative review, Figma was usually the better surface. For interaction-heavy workflows, a working prototype made behaviour, states and end-to-end flows easier to evaluate. I moved between them depending on which helped me make the next decision faster.

Prototypes were reviewed like any other product work — through Figma comments or direct walkthroughs — and lived in GitHub rather than on one person’s machine, so feedback could move back into the build without getting lost. Not every comment became a change, and not every AI-generated change was accepted.

“Automation shortened the feedback loop.
It didn’t remove the designer from it.”

What changed

The output changed.
My responsibility didn’t.

AI reduced the distance between an idea and something I could actually use. But deciding what to build, how an experience should behave, whether an AI-generated solution made sense, and when something needed manual design judgement still remained my responsibility.
