Figma handoff

How I used Claude Code, our design system and GitHub to move from requirements to working product experiences.

While working on newer BAIS experiences — including a candidate-facing ATS rebuild — our design process moved beyond static screens. Instead of designing the full experience in Figma and handing it over, AI-assisted coding became part of the design process itself.

The shift

Static prototypes weren't always enough.

Moving from reviewing an intended experience to interacting with a functioning one — not Agents becoming the ATS, a shift in design medium.

Where it started

Claude wasn't the starting point.
The product problem was.

Before starting a new experience, we first structured the product and UX requirements — for larger projects, a tailored UX requirements document (a UXRD) describing the experience we were trying to create.

A UXRD could include product context, users and personas, the jobs they need to accomplish, key journeys, requirements, important states, constraints and expected behaviour.

Design system as context

The next challenge was consistency.

Generating an interface with AI is easy. Generating something that actually belongs inside an existing enterprise product is much harder. We already had an established design system with reusable components in Storybook, connected to our codebase — so Claude could work with the patterns that already existed instead of inventing new ones.

“Create a blue button.”
“Design a table.”
“Use the existing Button component.”
“Work from the table patterns already available.”

Three different workflows — user access, role governance, conversational flow logic. Same tables, same cards, same navigation language.

Real project

Reimagining an ATS with Claude

Most applicant tracking systems treat every new candidate as inventory to clear. For a hiring manager running several open roles at once, that queue hides the one decision that actually needs them today. I rebuilt the experience around attention instead of inventory — designed, built and used end to end with Claude Code.

Understand→ Structure→ Design→ Build→ Use→ Critique→ Iterate

The transformation

Requirements became something I could click through.

The destination was never a generated screen. It was a working experience I could actually open, use and evaluate like a real product.

Division of labour

What Claude carried, and what stayed mine.

Where AI helped

From idea to something I could use. Claude turned a structured brief into a running interface fast enough that I was interacting with it the same day, not reviewing it a sprint later.

Where it still needed me

Working software isn't necessarily good UX. Whether a recommendation felt trustworthy, where control had to stay explicit, what counted as enough evidence — those calls stayed mine.

Design moments

Build → use → notice → change.

Using the working experience surfaced product decisions that are hard to see in a static screen. Five of them, below.

01Notification → prioritization

Instead of treating new applicants as inventory, the experience surfaces what deserves attention today and explains why — three candidates with strong evidence, three interviews blocking the next step, one decision waiting on a finalist.

Attention first
Ranked by what needs a decision, not recency
Why it surfaced
The reason sits next to the item, not hidden behind it
Role context
Every card is scoped to the role it belongs to
02Candidate profile → evidence workspace

AI recommendations needed to stay inspectable and challengeable rather than becoming an unexplained score — so a “strong match” verdict is backed by per-criterion evidence, sourced and ready to validate.

Evidence
Strongest signals named, not just scored
Needs validation
What the model is unsure about, stated plainly
Source access
Every claim links back to where it came from
Explicit action
Advance, hold or reject — never automatic
03
Conversation and filters operate the same query.

Natural-language exploration shouldn't silently change the system. The UI exposes the resulting filters so the person stays in control of what changed.

04
Human control doesn't mean manual execution.

Claude can interpret and prepare an action, but a consequential one stays visible and deliberate — previewed, then explicitly confirmed.

05
The assistant needed rules, not just prompts.

Scope, confidence and grounding had to be designed explicitly — what to do with ambiguity, missing evidence, or a disagreement with Priya's own read.

Feedback loop

Then we connected feedback back into the build.

Once prototype screens were brought into Figma, the team could review them normally and leave comments. We created a workflow that allowed Claude to read the relevant Figma feedback — so feedback could move back into the implementation loop without manually translating every small comment into another long instruction.

Working Prototype

Claude Reads Feedback
Implement Changes
Review Again

Claude could help interpret and implement feedback, but I still reviewed the result and decided whether the change actually solved the UX problem — not every comment was automatically accepted and shipped.

“Automation shortened the feedback loop.
It didn't remove the designer from it.”

Working across GitHub

The prototype wasn't trapped on my machine.

We maintained the work through GitHub. Designers could work through branches, push their latest changes, and let another designer or collaborator pull the current version and continue from there — so the prototype behaved more like a shared product artifact than a disposable one.

Claude reduced the distance between an idea and something I could actually use. But deciding what to build, how the experience should behave, whether an AI-generated solution made sense, and when something needed manual design judgement still remained my responsibility.

AI didn't remove design from the process
It removed some of the distance between designing and building.

Not a designer who makes Figma, hands it to engineering, and waits — but one who can understand, structure, design, build with AI, use, review, iterate and share. AI-generated output still needs human product and design judgement to become a real experience.
